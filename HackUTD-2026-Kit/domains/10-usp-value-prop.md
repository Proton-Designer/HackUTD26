# Unique Selling Proposition / Value Proposition

## Use this when
- Hour 1-2: comparing 2-4 candidate ideas and someone asks which one is actually different, not just slightly better.
- Writing the one-liner, Devpost tagline, poster headline, or the first 10 seconds of the pitch.
- A judge, mentor, or teammate asks "how is this different from X?" or "isn't this just ChatGPT/Notion/Google?"
- Hour 12 midpoint: checking whether what was built still matches the claim, then locking the message.
- Someone wants to add a feature, a buzzword, or a second persona to make the project stand out.
- Picking the one number the demo must prove on stage.

## Core principles
1. **Every claim becomes a measured fact with its comparison in the same sentence.** "Easy" and "fast" are dismissed; a stopwatch time, a count, or a user quote is hard to refute, and "5 minutes versus an hour of copy-paste" names the yardstick. [Obviously Awesome, p.41, p.67-68; p.39] [Seed: 0. Synthesis, §4]
2. **The competitor is what the user does today, usually a spreadsheet, manual work, a group chat, or nothing.** (counterintuitive) Users judge "better" against their current habit and have never heard of rival startups; beating a rival they do not use convinces no one. [Obviously Awesome, p.39, p.63-65]
3. **Win by a large margin on one dimension the user cares about, not slightly on many.** Slightly-better-everywhere does not differentiate, and audiences cannot judge what is not outstanding; collapse value to 1-4 themes and often just one. [Value Proposition Design, pp.160-161; Obviously Awesome, p.71]
4. **Pass the onliness test: "the ONLY [category] that [differentiator] for [whom]".** If three teams at the hackathon could say the same sentence, it is not a USP; list everyone who could claim it and move away from them. [Zag, p.52] [Seed: B. Core frameworks, §B1]
5. **Build the customer profile before the value map, rank it, and attack only the top 1-2 pains.** Mapping with your solution in mind lists only the pains you can already fix; a value map that claims to address everything is dishonest about the profile. [Value Proposition Design, pp.84-85, 93-94, 111-114] [Seed: B. Core frameworks, §B2]
6. **Pick the category frame on purpose, and borrow a known one ("X for Y") instead of inventing one.** (counterintuitive) The label brings assumed competitors and assumed features; a new category needs teaching time a 4-minute judge visit does not have. [Obviously Awesome, p.25-33, p.42-43, p.85-96]
7. **State the destination, not the vehicle: "users go from ___ to ___ in ___".** Describing the product type invites comparison to cheaper versions of it; walk each feature through feature, benefit, value ("so what?") until it reaches a user goal. [$100M Offers, p.78-79; Obviously Awesome, p.69-70]
8. **Differentiate on time and effort pushed toward zero, and put the proof on screen.** (counterintuitive) Bigger-outcome claims are the easiest to make and copy; speed and ease are harder, and invisible backend gains count for nothing until a counter, live result, or before/after shows them. [$100M Offers, p.62-63, p.63, p.68-69]
9. **Name a narrow user by findable traits, not demographics.** (counterintuitive) "People who do X at least N times a week" can be found and convinced; broad targeting makes it harder to persuade anyone you are their best choice, and the wider the competition, the narrower the focus must be. [Obviously Awesome, p.72-75; Zag, p.56]
10. **A trend comes after the category and must change the result.** "AI-powered" is not a category; use "[trend]-powered [familiar category] for [persona]" only if the trend visibly changes what the user gets, otherwise move it to the stack footnote. [Obviously Awesome, p.43-45, p.98-101]
11. **Claim only the fit you have, backed by actions rather than praise.** In 24 hours you can claim problem-solution fit and perhaps early traction; an email signup or booked follow-up is stronger evidence than "I'd use this". [Value Proposition Design, pp.123-126; pp.521, 525-528]
12. **Subtract to sharpen: one claim, and cut what does not serve it.** Adding features or message elements to stand out among ~200 projects is fighting clutter with more clutter; every feature and demo beat must help the onliness statement. [Zag, p.16-17, p.54, p.57]

## Decision procedure
Main decision: which differentiator to stake the project on, and the exact words to say it. Run first at hour 1-2 per candidate idea (about 30 minutes), then lock at hour 12.

1. **List real alternatives (10 min).** Ask 2-3 people who fit the target user "What do you do today when X?" and "What would you use if this did not exist?" Write their literal answers, rank by frequency, group into 2-3 clusters. Go: at least one cluster is a workaround (manual, spreadsheet, chat, nothing). Stop: if nobody has a workaround or cares, question whether the need is real. [Obviously Awesome, p.63-65; Zag, p.34]
2. **Rank the profile (15 min).** Jobs (functional, emotional, social), pains, gains, each scored 1-5 from the user's view; keep the top 3 of each. Make pains measurable ("how long is too long?"). [Seed: A. Hackathon value prop kit, §1; Value Proposition Design, pp.61-74]
3. **Filter the target pain on four questions:** important, tangible (felt now, so a judge can feel it in the demo), unsatisfied by current alternatives, lucrative (many people have it or a few pay a lot). Stop: drop any pain that fails "tangible". [Value Proposition Design, pp.226-235]
4. **List unique attributes against the alternatives only,** then a 3-column table (feature, benefit, value) for the top 3. The value column with a number is the headline candidate. [Obviously Awesome, p.66-70]
5. **Pick one dimension and estimate the gap** versus the top alternative (minutes, steps, errors). Aim for the "10x, not 10%" the trigger questions ask for; if your honest estimate is under about 2x, the dimension will not read as different on stage, so pick another dimension or narrow the segment until one is large. [Seed: B. Core frameworks, §B2; Value Proposition Design, pp.160-161]
6. **Run onliness.** Write "the only [category] that [differentiator] for [whom]". List who else could say it (other likely HackUTD teams, the dominant product). Go: no plausible other claimant. Otherwise narrow the user or task and retry; after two narrowing rounds with no clean claim, move to the next idea. [Zag, p.52, p.56]
7. **Choose the frame.** Write 2-3 candidate frames and pick the one that makes the standout feature look natural, not the one that sounds biggest. List features that frame assumes; for each missing one, pick another frame or say "deliberately does not do Y" in one sentence. [Obviously Awesome, p.25-33, p.42-43]
8. **Write the forms from one source:** one-liner of 12 words or fewer; "from ___ to ___ in ___"; the ad-lib sentence ("helps [segment] who want to [job] by [reliever] and [creator], unlike [alternative]") in 3-5 variants; headline + sub + 3 measurable bullets. [Zag, p.44; $100M Offers, p.78-79; Value Proposition Design, pp.191-192; Seed: A. Hackathon value prop kit, §4]
9. **Test comprehension (10 min).** Say the one-liner once to 2-3 people outside the team; ask them to repeat what it is, for whom, and why it helps. Go: at least 2 of 3 get all three unprompted. "I don't get it" means fix the words; "not useful" means fix the idea. [Seed: A. Hackathon value prop kit, §5; Building a StoryBrand, p.48; Zag, p.30-32]
10. **Lock at hour 12.** Have a teammate who did not build the core describe the product in one sentence; if it differs from the plan, pitch what exists. Then freeze one master doc (one-liner, 3 value points, proof per point, 5 likely judge questions with answers) that every slide, README, and Devpost field copies from. [Obviously Awesome, p.26-27, p.51, p.109]

## Checklists
**Statement check (run on every one-liner, tagline, or headline)**
- [ ] Names a specific user by trait or context, not "everyone", "students", or "businesses".
- [ ] Benefit is an outcome, not a list of technologies or features.
- [ ] "Unlike" names an alternative the user actually uses today, not "legacy systems".
- [ ] Contains a number or a concrete proof the demo shows.
- [ ] No "and" joining two promises; one proposition only. [Zag, p.68-69]
- [ ] A competitor or another team could not say the same sentence.
- [ ] Any trend word changes the user's result; otherwise removed.
(Base list: [Seed: A. Hackathon value prop kit, Quality checks])

**Proof check (before the pitch is final)**
- [ ] One measured number per claim, with sample size ("in our test of 20 cases"). [$100M Offers, p.118-119]
- [ ] Baseline timed on the user's real alternative, ideally shown side by side on stage. [$100M Offers, p.66]
- [ ] The proof is visible on screen within 5 seconds (counter, before/after, live result). [$100M Offers, p.63]
- [ ] Fit claimed at the stage reached ("problem-solution fit, N interviews, M signups"), never "market validated". [Value Proposition Design, pp.123-126]
- [ ] Mocked or Wizard-of-Oz parts disclosed if asked. [Value Proposition Design, pp.546-553]
- [ ] No promised medical, financial, or legal outcome; phrased as "in our test". [$100M Offers, p.128]

**Consistency check (hour 20-23)**
- [ ] Same phrase, visual motif, and demo moment on poster, slides, Devpost, README, and spoken pitch. [Zag, p.70]
- [ ] All 4 teammates can say the one-liner and the alternative-unique-value chain from memory. [Obviously Awesome, p.103-105]

## Anti-patterns
- **Feature list as value.** Tell: the benefit slot reads "AI, automation, integrations". Fix: run feature, benefit, value until the value column has a user goal and a number. [Seed: B. Core frameworks, §B5; Obviously Awesome, p.69-70]
- **Imaginary competitor.** Tell: "unlike legacy systems" or "unlike other apps". Fix: name the workaround users described in step 1. [Seed: B. Core frameworks, §B5; Obviously Awesome, p.39]
- **Fighting the category leader head-on.** Tell: the pitch is "a better/cheaper ChatGPT (or Notion)". Fix: pre-write the answer to the leader comparison as the niche need it ignores, then show that need. [Obviously Awesome, p.81-85, p.89-90]
- **Declaring a new category.** Tell: pitch time spent defining a coined term or acronym. Fix: borrow a familiar category and state the twist. [Obviously Awesome, p.92-96]
- **Buzzword without a link.** Tell: "agents" or "blockchain" in the name but no demo moment where it changes the result. Fix: ask what it concretely does for the user; if nothing, demote it to the stack. [Obviously Awesome, p.98, p.100-101]
- **Cosmetic niche.** Tell: "X for [group]" where the group would use it no differently (the pink pen "for her"). Fix: ask two members of the group what they would do differently; if no concrete answer, change idea or group. [Obviously Awesome, p.88]
- **More features to stand out.** Tell: "one more feature" proposed at hour 16 to differentiate. Fix: run the sacrifice game; strike everything that does not serve the onliness statement. [Zag, p.16-17, p.57]
- **Vague adjectives.** Tell: "seamless", "easy", "revolutionary", "innovative". Fix: replace each with a timed or counted fact. [Seed: 0. Synthesis, §4; Obviously Awesome, p.67-68]
- **Wording polish over a broken core.** Tell: hour 20 spent on taglines while the demo path still fails. Fix: make the one demo path reliable first; no claim rescues a core that does not work. [$100M Offers, p.123]
- **Overclaimed validation.** Tell: "users love it" from friendly praise. Fix: report actions taken (signups, follow-ups) with counts. [Value Proposition Design, pp.525-528]

## Hackathon calibration
- **Judges spend about 4 minutes and see ~200 projects.** The real competition is clutter: a judge must be able to say what, who, and why after 5 seconds, and remember it during deliberation. Open the demo on the user's finished result, not an empty state or login. [Zag, p.16-17; Seed: 0. Synthesis, §2; $100M Offers, p.65-66]
- **Map to HackUTD criteria.** The onliness claim serves "uniqueness"; the measured gap serves "usefulness" and "impact"; honest fit staging plus one segment-size number serves "viability". Spend roughly 80% of the pitch on today's problem and demo and 20% on scale or business model. [Obviously Awesome, p.52, p.75]
- **Sponsor tracks have a second buyer.** Write a separate one-line pain and gain for the sponsor as decision maker, and make the sponsor's tech deliver the differentiating dimension, not a side feature. Keep one overall narrative across tracks. [Value Proposition Design, pp.127-131; $100M Offers, p.114-115]
- **Positioning timeline compresses.** Keep the persona as a hypothesis for the first 6-8 hours while testing with varied people, then lock by hour 12 so all build work serves one story. [Obviously Awesome, p.49-51]
- **Prefer done-for-you.** Between a dashboard that advises and an agent that completes one real action end to end, the second carries more perceived value. [$100M Offers, p.67] (opinion-strength source)
- **Ignore for this event:** business-model fit, loyalty programs, brand extension, pricing tiers, scarcity and urgency mechanics, and statistically significant A/B tests. A 24-hour sample never reaches significance; report any headline comparison as directional with its sample size. Fake scarcity ("only 10 spots") is checkable by judges, so use it only if literally true. [Value Proposition Design, pp.564-568; $100M Offers, p.106]
- **Wizard of Oz is fine for the long tail.** Build the core path for real, hand-feed the rest, and disclose it when asked; judges punish discovered fakery more than declared scope. [$100M Offers, p.89-90; Value Proposition Design, pp.546-553]

## Where experts disagree
- **Write a positioning statement or not.** The seeds offer Moore's "For/who/is a/that/unlike" template; Dunford says never use it as the method because it records untested assumptions. Follow Dunford for process: do steps 1-7 first, then fill the template as a check that "unlike X" is real. [Seed: B. Core frameworks, §B5; Obviously Awesome, p.35-38]
- **Create a category or join one.** Neumeier says if you would rank fourth or lower, create a new category and be first in minds; Dunford calls category creation a last resort needing time and money. Follow Dunford in a 4-minute pitch: use a known category specialized for a narrow group, which can still be phrased as "the first [X] for [Y]". [Zag, p.49-50; Obviously Awesome, p.85-96]
- **Narrow early or broad early.** Hormozi, Neumeier, and Osterwalder push a hyper-specific user from the start; Dunford warns that premature narrowing hides the best fit. Follow both on a clock: broad testing until hour 6-8, single persona locked by hour 12. [$100M Offers, p.127; Zag, p.56; Obviously Awesome, p.51]
- **Preemptive claim versus provable uniqueness.** Reeves says the first to state a common but unspoken virtue owns it (steam-washed bottles); Dunford wants attributes truly unique against alternatives. Follow Dunford for the headline; use a preemptive claim only as a secondary line, and only if true and demonstrable, since judges compare neighboring projects directly. [Seed: B. Core frameworks, §B1; Obviously Awesome, p.66-68]
- **Functional speed versus emotional identity.** Hormozi says compete on time and effort; Neumeier and Osterwalder say identity, tribe, and emotional or social jobs often matter more. Follow the seed rule: the headline is one measured functional gap, plus one emotional line (the internal problem or a From/To identity). [$100M Offers, p.62-63; Zag, p.34; Value Proposition Design, p.57, p.85; Seed: B. Core frameworks, §B4; Building a StoryBrand, pp.54-56]
- **Trust tester reactions or not.** Seeds and Miller say rewrite if outsiders cannot repeat it; Neumeier says distinctive ideas test poorly on preference. Both hold if separated: test comprehension (can they repeat it?) and act on it; discount preference ("it's weird") when testers who understand it value it strongly. [Seed: A. Hackathon value prop kit, §5; Zag, p.30-32]
- **How many trends.** Neumeier says ride more than one; Dunford says add one only after the category and only if it links to the product. Follow Dunford: one trend, one datapoint, in the why-now line. [Zag, p.35-36; Obviously Awesome, p.97-101]
