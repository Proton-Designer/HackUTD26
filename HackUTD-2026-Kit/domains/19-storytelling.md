# Technical and persuasive storytelling and communication

## Use this when
- Hour 1-2: the idea is chosen and the team needs one sentence (ABT plus headline) that also works as the scope veto filter.
- Hour 16-20: drafting the table pitch for science-fair judging (~4 minutes per judge, ~200 projects) and choosing the one demo flow.
- Deciding the order of problem, demo, and tech explanation, and how much architecture goes in the spoken pitch versus Q&A and Devpost.
- Choosing which 2-3 numbers, which quote, and which STAR moment survive into the pitch.
- Writing the Devpost write-up and video so they tell the same story as the spoken pitch.
- A rehearsal listener cannot say back what the project is, or praises it as "dense."

## Core principles
1. **Anchor on one named user in one specific moment, before any UI.** Emotion does not scale with headcount: a single child's story raised $2.38 per donor versus $1.14 for statistics alone [Made to Stick, p.151-153]; pick one of three personas and tell hers in depth [Houston, We Have a Narrative, pp.40-42].
2. **Make judges agree on the problem before they see the product.** A shared question syncs an audience: narrative clips produced about 70% inter-subject brain correlation versus 10-20% for non-narrative ones [Houston, pp.43-45]. Spend about 30% of pitch time on the problem and demo nothing until a judge could state it [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.120-122, p.138].
3. **Write the spine as And-But-Therefore; a feature list is not a story.** "And, and, and" is a list, "but" without "therefore" is complaining, ABT is a story [Houston, p.21, p.45]. If nobody can finish "the problem is ___" in one sentence, the pitch is a list.
4. **One core message, three supporting points, never more than five.** "If you say three things, you don't say anything" [Made to Stick, p.36-37]; groups past three to five are forgotten unless chunked under a stated idea [Minto, p.23-24].
5. **Demo the hardest real input and let the judge drive it (counterintuitive).** One demanding case proves more than a success rate (Sinatra Test) and a claim judges verify themselves outsources credibility to them [Made to Stick, p.138-145]; experienced points stick for years [Made to Stick, p.104-106]. The instinct is a clean toy input run by the team.
6. **Person first, numbers second, and every number human-scaled with a baseline (counterintuitive).** Adding statistics to the story cut giving from $2.38 to $1.43 [Made to Stick, p.151-153]. Translate to per-day or per-person units: the same accuracy framed in human scale scored 83% "very impressive" versus 58% [Made to Stick, p.131-134]. Lead with market size and you lose both effects.
7. **Explain the tech after the demo, as the answer to "how?" (counterintuitive for engineers).** Each point should raise the question the next one answers; an architecture slide before the result answers a question nobody has asked yet [Minto, p.34-36]. Show the person and the result first, then reveal how [Resonate, p.107, p.112-115]. Layer it: one sentence for everyone, more on request [Made to Stick, p.56-57].
8. **Phrase key lines as contrasts: before versus after, "from X to Y."** About half of the applause in 476 political speeches followed a contrast [Resonate, p.104]. Show the old way and your way side by side on the same task, then stay silent [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.242, p.252-253].
9. **Open a gap, have the judge commit to a guess, close it with the live result.** Curiosity is the pain of a knowledge gap [Made to Stick, p.80-84]; people who predict publicly are more curious when shown wrong [Made to Stick, p.72-73]. Ask: "How long do you think this takes by hand?"
10. **Use one headline of 140 characters or fewer, in subject-verb-object form, repeated word for word.** Say it at the open, after the demo, and at the close, and put it on the slide, Devpost title, and repo [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.84-90; Talk Like TED, p.105-107]. Every teammate says the same words.
11. **Claim only what you measured, and name the limits yourself.** Olson ties exaggerated causal claims (33% of press releases) to "storytelling run amok" [Houston, pp.14-16]; claims you do not believe leak through body language [Talk Like TED, p.74]. Say "3 of 4 test cases," never "revolutionary."
12. **Close on the user's resolved after-state plus one concrete ask.** Reuse the opening scene with the result changed; open endings shrink the audience [Houston, p.53, pp.159-161]. End on the future state, not "thank you, any questions" [Resonate, p.66-68], and never on a trial close like "so, what do you think?" [Pitch Anything, p.62].

## Decision procedure
Main decision: what story the pitch tells, and in what order.

1. **Start a story log at hour 0.** One line per real incident: failed approach, surprising bug, first user reaction, exact quotes [Made to Stick, p.201-202]. Never invent drama.
2. **Lock the protagonist and problem (hour 1-2).** Write one named user, one task, one failure moment. Go only if all four teammates finish "the problem is ___" with the same sentence. If they disagree, ask Olson's question, "what is the problem at the center?", before building more [Houston, p.45, p.153].
3. **Write the ABT.** Each teammate writes one on their own in 5 minutes, then merge [Houston, p.21]. Accept it only when the BUT is a real, checkable problem and the THEREFORE is your product. Then fill the seed logline: "For [who], [Product] helps [job] by [distinct approach], so they can [outcome]" [Seed: Hackathon story kit, §A.1].
4. **Write the headline.** Draft 3-5, at most 140 characters each. Test on 3 people outside the team after a single hearing. Keep the one they repeat back. If none survives, the core is not clear yet: go back to step 3 [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.92-98].
5. **Pick three messages (problem, demo, proof) and cut "news."** For every feature, stat, or tech detail, ask "which of the three does this support?" If the answer is none, drop it however cool it is [Minto, p.89-90].
6. **Choose the opening order.** If the problem is familiar, go headline, short problem, demo early. If your insight is counterintuitive ("the real problem isn't X"), give the short argument before the demo [Minto, p.83-85]. Either way, the first sentence names the person, the pain, and the payoff, with no stack names [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.49-51].
7. **Choose exactly one STAR moment** [Resonate, p.148-149]. Your options, best first: a judge types their own input; your ugliest real input runs live; a side-by-side of old and new on the same task. It must reinforce the core and make sense in hindsight. Cut any stunt that teaches nothing [Made to Stick, p.67-69]. Pre-compute anything that takes over 15 seconds [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.243-246].
8. **Pick the proof.** Use at most 2-3 numbers in the whole pitch [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.202]. Each one is something you measured, has a stated baseline, and is converted to human units. Add one real user quote, verbatim, with permission.
9. **Fit the clock.** With ~4 minutes per judge, the spoken pitch targets 2:00 and must stay under 2:30, leaving at least 90 seconds for questions. At 150-190 words per minute [Talk Like TED, p.68-70], a 2:00 script is 300-380 words. The seed beats are planned for 2:50 [Seed: Hackathon story kit, §A.3]; compress them to: hook (person and moment) 0:00-0:15; stakes with one number 0:15-0:30; headline and after-state 0:30-0:40; demo with the STAR moment 0:40-1:35; one-sentence "how," naming the technical centerpiece and the sponsor tech, 1:35-1:50; proof and close with the ask 1:50-2:05.
10. **Build the ladder from the same spine.** Logline, then the four-sentence 30-second version (what you do, what problem, how you are different, why I care [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.134-135]), then the 2:00 pitch, then Devpost, then the video [Seed: Synthesis playbook, §0.8]. Keep the 30-second version ready to jump to when a judge's attention drops [Pitch Anything, p.48-49].
11. **Gate on outsider tests.** A non-team listener hears 90 seconds and is asked only "what was it about, and what was confusing?" [Houston, p.189]. Go only if they state the problem and the headline. Then score SUCCESs. A "no" on Simple or Concrete blocks the pitch until fixed [Made to Stick, p.200-202].
12. **Rehearse by cutting.** Do at least 5 timed run-throughs (target 10 [Talk Like TED, p.63-64]). Each run fixes exactly one thing a listener names [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.311-312]. Cut about a third on the first run and save the cut material for Q&A [Made to Stick, p.36, p.41-44].

## Checklists
**Pitch script (run before every rehearsal)**
- [ ] The first sentence has the person, the pain, and the payoff. There is no team intro and no tech stack.
- [ ] One named protagonist. Her name also appears in the demo sample data [Made to Stick, p.116-119].
- [ ] A clear BUT. Reading the script aloud, no "and then" or "also" chains [Houston, p.20].
- [ ] "You/they" outnumber "we/our" [Building a StoryBrand, p.41].
- [ ] Exactly one STAR moment, with a rehearsed fallback (video or screenshots).
- [ ] At most 3 numbers, each measured, with a baseline, in human units, said after the emotional beat.
- [ ] The architecture comes after the demo, in at most 15 seconds, naming the technical centerpiece.
- [ ] The headline is said at least 3 times, word for word.
- [ ] The close returns to the opening scene, resolved, and gives one concrete ask ("try it at this QR code").
- [ ] 300-380 words, timed under 2:30.

**Devpost write-up** [Seed: Hackathon story kit, §A.4]
- [ ] The tagline is the headline. The first line of "What it does" is the logline.
- [ ] Inspiration names the real person and the problem, never "we wanted to use X."
- [ ] How we built it: 3-5 bullets plus one diagram, sponsor tech named explicitly, mocked parts labeled.
- [ ] Challenges: 1-2 real technical problems and how you solved them (CTO judges read this).
- [ ] What we learned is specific, because MLH judges score learning.
- [ ] Section headings state claims, not labels like "Overview" [Minto, p.50, p.60].
- [ ] Every claim matches the spoken pitch, slides, and video.

**Claims audit**
- [ ] Every superlative ("unique," "revolutionary," "game-changing") is replaced with a fact [Made to Stick, p.156-161].
- [ ] Every number can answer "compared to what?" [Made to Stick, p.134-135].
- [ ] Small samples are stated as counts ("all 5 testers"), not percentages.
- [ ] One teammate spends 10 minutes trying to break the headline claim [Houston, pp.195-196].

## Anti-patterns
- **The "and, and, and" feature tour.** Tell: the outsider says back a list of features, not a problem. Fix: rebuild from the ABT and demo one continuous scenario [Houston, p.45; Made to Stick, p.100-102].
- **The "about us" opener.** Tell: the first sentence starts "We are team X, we built Y using Z." Fix: open on the problem moment or the most surprising fact [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.58-62].
- **Architecture before the result.** Tell: a stack diagram appears before the judge has seen anything work. Fix: move it after the demo as the "how?" answer, or use Duarte's cliffhanger: pause the story at its peak, explain in 30 seconds, then show the payoff [Resonate, p.114].
- **Stats inside the emotional beat.** Tell: "Maria loses 40 minutes a shift, and 3.2 million nurses..." Fix: put the person first and the aggregate number in a later beat [Made to Stick, p.151-153].
- **The slideument.** Tell: bullets or more than about 7 words on a slide, and the presenter reads them. Fix: one idea and one picture per slide, with the details in Devpost [Resonate, p.50-51; Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.156-165].
- **Gimmick surprise.** Tell: judges remember the joke or prop but cannot name the product. Fix: the surprise must break an assumption about the core [Made to Stick, p.67-70].
- **"Dense" taken as praise.** Tell: a listener says "a lot to process" or "works on many levels." Fix: treat it as overload and cut [Houston, p.187].
- **The needy or open close.** Tell: the pitch ends with "any questions?", "hope you like it," or "future work." Fix: state what works today and the next step, then stop talking [Pitch Anything, p.62, p.116-117].
- **Inflated stakes.** Tell: "billions suffer" with no source. Fix: one sourced, specific consequence; over-urgency reads as manipulation [Resonate, p.68, p.78-79].
- **Citing the 7-38-55 statistic.** Tell: claiming "93% of communication is nonverbal." Fix: never use it; the researcher says it was taken out of context [Talk Like TED, p.72-74].

## Hackathon calibration
- **Clock.** Klaff's pitch is designed for 20 minutes [Pitch Anything, p.89-90], and Jobs and Duarte plan for keynotes. A ~4-minute judge slot is a 2:00 pitch. Duarte's 3-5 what-is/what-could-be oscillations shrink to one or two, with the demo as the main "could be" [Resonate, p.58-63]. The 10-minute attention rule matters only for a finals stage slot [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.152-153].
- **Judges are repeat-exposure and tired.** By your table they have seen dozens of projects. The first 15 seconds must show the problem and a visual with no jargon [Pitch Anything, p.16-19], and the line you want repeated is the headline.
- **Wow moments are small.** Jobs planned his reveals for months. Here the STAR moment is a judge's own input or real data appearing live, rehearsed, with a fallback [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.262-278].
- **Proof is collected during the event.** StoryBrand's testimonials and logos become 2-3 user quotes gathered on site plus one measured before/after [Building a StoryBrand, p.64-68].
- **Ignore the brand material.** Miller's website, brand, and organization chapters apply only to the Devpost hero line and the landing screen [Building a StoryBrand, p.112-118].
- **Soften Klaff.** Keep his stated time budget, no neediness, and the prepared build-night intrigue story under 45 seconds [Pitch Anything, p.53-57]. Drop the dominance and walk-out tactics with student and sponsor judges [Pitch Anything, p.108-110].
- **Sponsor tracks.** Reuse the sponsor's product names, not their marketing vocabulary [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.212-213]. Frame impact in terms of the sponsor's mission or identity [Made to Stick, p.170-180; Resonate, p.58-59]. Say what their tech made possible that was not possible before.
- **Rehearsal budget.** Olson took 16 years to make story intuitive. You have 2-3 hours: at least 5 timed runs, one recorded run, and one run with an outsider. Freeze features first.

## Where experts disagree
- **Answer first or problem first?** Minto says lead with the conclusion [Minto, p.25-27]. Olson and Gallo say build the problem before the solution [Houston, pp.43-45; Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.120-122]. Follow both, in layers: the first sentence is the headline (the answer), the next 20-30 seconds build the problem, then the demo proves it. In Q&A and with CTO judges, go pure Minto: answer, then the 2-3 supports [Seed: Making it stick and clear, §C2].
- **Reveal everything in 30 seconds, or hold a curiosity gap?** Minto wants the whole message up front [Minto, p.49, p.59]. Klaff and the Heaths withhold the resolution [Pitch Anything, p.58-59; Made to Stick, p.76-79]. Follow this split: state the claim early and withhold the proof. The live result closes the gap. Keep the deep "how" for questions so the judge asks for it [Pitch Anything, p.106].
- **Open with a shift in the world or with one person?** Raskin opens on a big change with winners and losers [Seed: Story structures, §B1]. Olson and the Heaths open on one person [Houston, pp.40-42; Made to Stick, p.151-153]. At a table, use the person; the evidence on identifiable individuals is stronger. Add one "why now" sentence (cost, behavior, or capability that just changed [Pitch Anything, p.92-96]) only when the new tech is the point, as on most sponsor tracks.
- **Pair every story with a number, or keep them apart?** Duarte says pair them [Resonate, p.100-103]. The Heaths' experiments show stats in the same moment suppress caring [Made to Stick, p.151-153]. Pair them, but in sequence: the story beat first, the number in the next beat.
- **How simple?** Olson and the Heaths say simple is sophisticated [Houston, pp.31-33]. Klaff warns that over-simplified pitches seem naive [Pitch Anything, p.101-102]. Simplify the statistics and the structure, but keep rich, specific detail about the person, and keep technical depth one question away.
- **Who presents?** Duarte suggests varying speakers [Resonate, p.138-139]. Gallo caps it at 2 for a short slot [Presentation Secrets of Steve Jobs, p.228-231]. Use 2: the storyteller and the demo driver. The other two own Q&A buckets.
