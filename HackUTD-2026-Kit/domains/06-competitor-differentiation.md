# Competitor research/analysis and identifying differentiation factors

## Use this when
- Hour 0-2: the team has 2-3 candidate ideas and needs to know which one is meaningfully different from what users already do, and from what earlier hackathon teams built.
- A search turns up an existing app, a past HackUTD/Devpost project, or the target sponsor's own product that looks like your idea.
- Someone frames the idea as "X but with AI" or "Notion for Y" and you must decide whether that is a real angle or a me-too.
- Hour 14-20: building the comparison table, value-curve slide, or Devpost "what makes it unique" section.
- Q&A prep: writing one-sentence answers to "how is this different from ___?", "who else does this?" and "what stops a big company copying you?"
- Another team at the venue appears to be building the same thing.

## Core principles
1. **Start from what the user does today, including doing nothing, not from rival startups.** Users judge "better" against their current workaround (spreadsheet, group chat, ChatGPT, manual work), and that workaround is usually the real competitor. [Obviously Awesome, p.39, p.63-64]; [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.91-97]
2. **Name competitors; never claim "no competitors" or shrink the market until you own it.** (counterintuitive) Judges hear "no competitors" constantly and read it as "did not look" or "no demand"; a set that omits the obvious alternative (ChatGPT, Google, the incumbent) costs credibility. [Zero to One, p.23-25]; [Crossing the Chasm, p.115, p.146]
3. **An attribute is unique only if the alternatives lack it; keep 2-4.** If two alternatives also have it, it is table stakes. One differentiator is too easy to copy, ten means nothing stands out. [Obviously Awesome, p.66-68]; [Seed: B. Positioning and differentiation, §B1]
4. **Be roughly 10x better on one dimension, measured against the real alternative.** A 20% gain is invisible; order-of-magnitude gaps (instant payment versus a 7-10 day check) escape comparison. Every claim names its yardstick in the same sentence ("5 minutes versus an hour of copy-paste"). [Zero to One, p.38-39]; [Obviously Awesome, p.39]
5. **Differentiate by subtracting as much as by adding.** (counterintuitive) Fill all four ERRC boxes (Eliminate, Reduce, Raise, Create). Only Raise and Create produces a costlier me-too; eliminations pay for the build time spent on creates. [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.40-43, p.58-60, p.65-66]
6. **Diverge in shape, not altitude.** If your value curve tracks the incumbents' at a slightly higher level ("a bit more for a bit less"), you are a me-too. Change at least one factor entirely. [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.55-56, p.81]; [Zero to One, p.20-28]
7. **Do not fight the category leader on its home ground; win a subsegment with a real need.** Framing as a general version of a known product invites "how is this better than the leader?" and you lose on breadth. "X for Y" works only if Y has a concrete, different need, not a relabel. [Obviously Awesome, p.81-91]; [Crossing the Chasm, p.85, p.98-101]
8. **Define competition with two references: a market alternative and a product alternative.** The market alternative is the budget or habit you displace; the product alternative is another newcomer on the same tech shift. Position at their intersection. If you cannot name both, the idea may lack a market. [Crossing the Chasm, p.118-133]
9. **A moat needs a Benefit and a Barrier; "we execute better" is not a Barrier.** Name what stops a competent rival copying it (data access, conflict with their revenue, niche too small for them). Process and learning curves are shared by everyone. [7 Powers, p.30-31, p.116-122]
10. **Turn every differentiation claim into proof you can show.** Replace "easier" or "smarter" with a stopwatch time, a test-set accuracy, or a quote from a real tester; others' evidence beats your own claims. [Obviously Awesome, p.41, p.67-68]; [Crossing the Chasm, p.141-143]
11. **Research informs positioning, not the roadmap; time-box it.** (counterintuitive) Teams fixated on rivals copy their features and converge on the same product. Challenge every shared feature: "would a user notice it missing in a 3-minute demo?" [Zero to One, p.31-33]; [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.13, p.41, p.59]
12. **Set the scoring criteria yourself.** In the pitch, state 2-3 criteria a good solution must meet that your strengths satisfy and the alternatives do not, using only criteria you can demo live. [Obviously Awesome, p.81, p.89, p.108-109]

## Decision procedure
Main decision: does this idea have a defensible differentiation story worth committing 24 hours to, and what exactly is it? Total budget about 70 minutes in hours 0-2, then freeze. [Seed: A. Hackathon quick-run protocol, §1-4]

1. **Ask before you search (10 min).** Ask 2-3 people who fit the target user: "What would you use or do if this did not exist?" Log literal answers. These are the first rows of the alternatives list. [Obviously Awesome, p.63-64]
2. **Alternatives list (15 min).** Reach 10-15 entries across direct, indirect (different solution, same problem), adjacent (same tech, different audience), and do-nothing. Search the problem in the user's words, not your product name: Product Hunt (newest and most upvoted), G2/Capterra, `site:reddit.com [problem]`, Show HN, YouTube "best [category] tools" videos with 50K+ views. Rank by how common each is for your user and cluster into 2-5 groups. [Seed: C. Doing the competitor research, §C1]; [Obviously Awesome, p.64-65]
3. **Prior-art sweep with stop rules (20 min).**
   - Devpost: search the idea and the sponsor challenge, plus past HackUTD galleries. Note what was built and what was missing.
   - If a polished product already solves the exact problem for the exact audience: STOP. Change the audience or angle, not just features. [Crossing the Chasm, p.85]
   - If the sponsor already sells it: extend or complement it, never re-pitch it. [Seed: A. Hackathon quick-run protocol, §2]
   - If ChatGPT/Claude does it in one prompt: differentiation must come from workflow, data, integration, or UX, and you must say what survives a model upgrade. [Seed: A. Hackathon quick-run protocol, §2]; [7 Powers, p.80]
   - If a team at the venue has a near-identical idea: re-niche within the first hours rather than racing head-on. [Zero to One, p.34]
4. **Review-gap mining (15 min).** Read 2-3 star reviews of the top 3 alternatives. List recurring complaints; pick 1-2 you can fix in 24h. Also time their key flow and note any strategic contradiction (claims easy, takes 9 clicks). [Seed: A. Hackathon quick-run protocol, §3]; [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.85]
5. **Name the two references.** Write the market alternative and the product alternative. If either is blank, find the substitute people use today or drop the idea. [Crossing the Chasm, p.131-133]
6. **Unique attributes filter.** List every attribute the alternatives lack (tech, data, delivery, team skill, sponsor access). Strike any held by 2+ alternatives. GO needs 2-4 survivors; 0 survivors means NO-GO or rework. For each survivor write attribute, then value in user terms, then the proof you will show. [Obviously Awesome, p.66-70]; [Seed: 0. Synthesis, §5-6]
7. **10x dimension.** Pick one measurable dimension (time, steps, cost, accuracy) and estimate current way versus yours. Near 10x: GO and make it the demo headline. Only incremental (around 20%): change the dimension or the idea. Name the route: new invention, radical improvement of one step, or integrated design. [Zero to One, p.38-39]
8. **Canvas and ERRC (20 min).** Score 3-5 alternatives (including do-nothing) on 5-7 factors named in buyer language, 1-5 each. Plot your curve. Require: at least one factor changed entirely (not just level), at least 1 entry per ERRC box, and cuts greater than or equal to additions. The Eliminate/Reduce list becomes the explicit not-building list. [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.54-60, p.65-66]
9. **Barrier check.** One line Benefit, one line Barrier. If the Barrier is "we will execute better", find a real one (counter-positioning against an incumbent's revenue, private sponsor data, a niche too small for the big player) or present it honestly as an early-lead story. [7 Powers, p.30-31, p.55-57]
10. **Go/no-go.** Score 1-5 on target user, compelling reason, complete end-to-end flow in 24h, and competition (not already owned). Any very low score eliminates the idea regardless of total. Then the litmus test: focus, divergence, and a true tagline. Fail the tagline and you reject, because judges give about one sentence of attention. [Crossing the Chasm, p.83-85]; [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.68-79]
11. **Freeze and produce artifacts.** One comparison table (you versus 3 alternatives, 4-6 rows), or one value-curve or 2x2 visual with you alone in a quadrant, plus a one-sentence answer per top alternative. Do not reopen research except for the pitch or if a step-3 stop rule newly fires. [Seed: A. Hackathon quick-run protocol, §5]

## Checklists

**A. Comparison table or slide**
- [ ] Includes do-nothing/manual and the general tool judges think of first (ChatGPT, Google, the incumbent). [Crossing the Chasm, p.115]
- [ ] 4-6 rows, named in buyer language; a non-technical person can restate each. [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.87]
- [ ] Rows grouped as must-haves, differentiators, honest weaknesses; you win the differentiator rows and do not fake the rest. [Seed: C. Doing the competitor research, §C4]
- [ ] Every row you claim to win can be shown live in the demo.
- [ ] Comparisons are like for like (same task, same input, same unit).
- [ ] Readable from across a science-fair table: two curves or one table, tagline underneath. [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.134, p.146]

**B. "How is this different from X?" answers**
- [ ] One prepared answer per top 3 alternative, each under 15 seconds. [Zero to One, p.23-24]
- [ ] Pattern: "X works well for A, but cannot do B because C; we do B, shown by D." Respectful, factual tone. [Crossing the Chasm, p.133]
- [ ] Names the real yardstick and a number. [Obviously Awesome, p.39]
- [ ] "What if Google/OpenAI builds this?" answer names why they would not or could not: incentives, data access, niche size, conflict with their cash cow. [7 Powers, p.31, p.59-61]; [Obviously Awesome, p.110-111]
- [ ] For AI projects: states what is retained if the underlying model improves (data, workflow, integrations). [7 Powers, p.80]

**C. Every differentiation claim**
- [ ] Unique versus the alternatives, not just versus nothing.
- [ ] Has a proof artifact collected before judging: stopwatch time, test-set result, or tester quote. [Obviously Awesome, p.67-68]
- [ ] No unearned moats: no "brand", no network effect without filling "each additional X makes it better for Y because Z", no "proprietary data" that fails the cornered-resource tests. [7 Powers, p.42-46, p.88, p.105-109]
- [ ] Labels what is live, simulated, and roadmap. [Crossing the Chasm, p.95-96]

## Anti-patterns
1. **"We have no competitors."** Tell: competitor slide empty or lists only obscure startups. Fix: list the current workaround first and name the one dimension where you are clearly better. [Zero to One, p.23-25]
2. **"X but with AI."** Tell: your canvas line runs parallel to the incumbent's. Fix: change a factor entirely, or ask which assumption the incumbent's workflow depends on that you can break (zero marginal cost, no human review step). [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.81]; [7 Powers, p.30, p.38]
3. **Feature-parity scoping.** Tell: the backlog contains features "because competitor has it". Fix: apply the 3-minute-demo test and move them to Eliminate/Reduce. [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.41, p.59]
4. **Rigged competitor set.** Tell: the market is defined as an intersection of niches ("British food in Palo Alto") or the obvious rival is missing. Fix: include what a skeptical judge would name first. [Zero to One, p.23-25]; [Crossing the Chasm, p.146]
5. **Cosmetic niche.** Tell: "for students" or "for nurses" with no change in flow, data, or vocabulary. Fix: ask two people in the group what they would do differently with it; if no concrete answer, change the idea or the group. [Obviously Awesome, p.88]
6. **Buzzword as differentiator.** Tell: "disruptive", "AI-powered", "blockchain" carry the slide. Fix: replace with the Benefit and Barrier sentences and the 10x number; demote the tech to the technical slide. [7 Powers, p.62]; [Obviously Awesome, p.98-101]
7. **Inventing a new category in a 4-minute pitch.** Tell: time spent explaining a coined term. Fix: borrow a known category and describe the twist. [Obviously Awesome, p.92-96]
8. **Attacking the incumbent.** Tell: the pitch is defined by who you oppose. Fix: lead with the user's outcome; mention incumbents only as the status quo you improve on. [Zero to One, p.43-44]; [7 Powers, p.63]
9. **Unbounded research.** Tell: hour 3 and still adding rows. Fix: hit the 70-minute budget, freeze, revisit only for the pitch. [Zero to One, p.31-33]
10. **Overclaiming completeness.** Tell: the comparison table ticks boxes the demo cannot show. Fix: narrow the promise until the live demo delivers 100% of it. [Crossing the Chasm, p.95-96]

## Hackathon calibration
- **Judges see about 200 projects for about 4 minutes each.** Uniqueness is scored against the room as much as against the market, so the Devpost and past-HackUTD sweep matters more than Crunchbase or SimilarWeb. Judges may have seen your idea last year; know what that team built and what they missed. [Seed: 0. Synthesis, §3]
- **The differentiator has to be visible in the demo, not argued.** Spend design effort on one Kano delighter that becomes the memorable moment; must-haves only need to work. [Seed: B. Positioning and differentiation, §B3]
- **Sponsor judges** know their own product line. Extend or complement it and show the sponsor technology doing something the alternatives cannot. **MLH track judges** score technology, design, completion, and learning, so a light differentiation story is fine there; do not divert build time for it.
- **Ignore or shrink long-horizon advice:** brand power, scale economies, switching costs, channel design, price corridors, and full category creation need months or years. A prototype has no scale and no brand. Replace "brand" with trust devices (visible sources, accuracy numbers, a real quote) and "scale" with one data point showing the mechanism starts. [7 Powers, p.35, p.88]; [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.198]
- **Viability questions still come.** Answer moat questions honestly: most hackathon software has no moat, so name the early advantage (niche focus, data, speed) instead of claiming IP. Give one pricing line anchored to the market alternative's cost. [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.195-198]; [Crossing the Chasm, p.153-156]
- **Prefer ideas useful to a single user on day one.** Network-effect ideas need a crowd the demo cannot provide. [Zero to One, p.39-40]; [7 Powers, p.44]
- **Validation scales down:** 5-6 short conversations across at least three user types in about an hour, observation over opinions, and a 2-minute dot vote from nearby teams or mentors on 2-3 canvases. [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.137-141]
- **Hour 12 drift check:** have a teammate who did not build the core describe it in one sentence; if it differs from the plan, reposition to what exists. [Obviously Awesome, p.26-27]

## Where experts disagree
- **Positioning statement or not.** Moore says write the two-sentence claim ("For [beachhead] dissatisfied with [market alternative], we are a [category] that [reason]. Unlike [product alternative], we have [features].") by hour 2 [Crossing the Chasm, p.138-141]. Dunford says filling in that template records untested assumptions and is useless as a method [Obviously Awesome, p.35-38]. Follow both in order: run Dunford's alternatives-to-value analysis first, then derive Moore's sentence as the output, because the pitch and Devpost still need one sentence.
- **How much attention to give rivals.** Thiel says cap research at 15-20 minutes and stop obsessing over competitors [Zero to One, p.31-33]. Kim and Mauborgne and Moore want a scored canvas and named references [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.54-56]; [Crossing the Chasm, p.118-131]. Follow the seed's ~70-minute structured pass once, then freeze. Use competitors in the comparison slide and Q&A, never as the roadmap or the pitch's opening.
- **Create a new space versus borrow a category.** Blue Ocean and Thiel push uncontested space and being different in kind. Dunford calls new-category creation a last resort, and Moore says buyers need a reference competitor to place you. Follow: make the value curve divergent, but frame it inside a known category with a stated twist. A 4-minute slot cannot teach a new category.
- **When to narrow the target.** Moore and Thiel want one beachhead chosen at hour 1 and committed to hard [Crossing the Chasm, p.76-77, p.86-87]; [Zero to One, p.41-42]. Dunford advises staying broad until you see who loves it [Obviously Awesome, p.51]. Follow Moore for build scope (24h leaves no room to wander), but treat the persona as a hypothesis until the hour-12 test sessions, then lock it.
- **Price level.** Blue Ocean says price low when protection is weak [Blue Ocean Strategy, p.198]; Moore says price at leader level to signal credibility [Crossing the Chasm, p.153-156]. This barely matters here; give one line anchored to the cost of the manual alternative.
