# Hackathon Strategy

## 1\. Understanding the Problem

* Understand the metrics  
* Understand how big the problem is  
* Understand how frequent it is  
* Understand how much money is being lost/burned due to that problem  
* Analyze currently available solutions

## 2\. Target Audience & Persona

* Define the customer persona / user you are building for  
* Drill down and narrow the audience focus  
* Formulate the foundation: *"These people experience \[effect\] from \[problem\], which is why we are building \[solution\]."*  
* Deep dive into audience characteristics, preferences, and limitations  
* Identify friction points at the root level of existing solutions

## 3\. Solution & Feature Definition

* Brainstorm unique solutions (limit features; avoid looking like a one-size-fits-all product)  
* Optimize your Unique Selling Proposition (USP) to eliminate friction and ensure a seamless experience  
* Evaluate features by asking:  
  * Who will use this?  
  * Is this solving the core problem or is it just nice to have?  
* Prioritize core features first; add optional features later  
* Avoid over-engineering or adding unnecessary technologies/stack complexity—stay focused on solving the problem

## 4\. Pitch Deck & Presentation Strategy

* Problem: *Engage judges with opening questions*  
* Evidence: *Provide clear proof of the problem*  
* Cost: *Highlight the financial or operational impact*  
* Solution: *Explain clearly without jargon so anyone can understand*  
* Demo: *Show a working prototype starting from the user's perspective; highlight key features*  
* Comparison: *Illustrate the "Before and After" state*  
* Architecture: *Briefly cover technical details and stack if time allows*  
* Team Slide: *Introduce team members and why you are uniquely qualified*

Q\&A Prep: *Know your USP and competitors; address missing features by noting time constraints and future roadmap*

# **Hackathon Strategy**

## **Objective**

Build and demonstrate a distinctive, useful, technically credible project that competes strongly for first place overall and first place in a selected sponsor challenge.

Make decisions based on three questions:

* What meaningful benefit does this create for the user?  
* Which judging criterion or sponsor requirement does this support?  
* What evidence will we show to prove it?

## **1\. Competition and Judging Alignment**

* Read the current rules, judging criteria, sponsor challenges, and submission requirements before committing to an idea.  
* Identify published judging weights, presentation limits, team restrictions, and prize eligibility.  
* Confirm which awards can be combined and how many challenges a project may enter.  
* Separate overall judging criteria from sponsor-specific criteria.  
* Translate each criterion into something demonstrable: working functionality, technical accomplishment, originality, design quality, or supported impact.  
* Identify required submission materials and assign an owner.  
* Prepare permitted tools, accounts, hardware, and development environments before the event, within its preparation rules.

Deliverable: A concise competition brief covering requirements, scoring criteria, target awards, and deadlines.

## **2\. Understanding the Problem**

* Define the specific problem and the situation in which it occurs.  
* Identify its root causes rather than treating only its symptoms.  
* Establish its frequency, severity, and consequences.  
* Quantify financial loss, wasted time, operational inefficiency, accessibility barriers, or other relevant effects where credible evidence exists.  
* Distinguish the total scale of the problem from the portion our solution can realistically address.  
* Analyze existing solutions and why the problem persists despite them.  
* Record sources and distinguish established facts from assumptions.  
* Avoid selecting a problem solely because it sounds dramatic or has a large market-size statistic.

Deliverable: A clearly defined problem supported by credible evidence and an explanation of the unresolved gap.

## **3\. Target Audience and User Workflow**

* Define the primary user and the exact task they need to accomplish.  
* Narrow the audience enough to make meaningful design decisions.  
* Distinguish the user from the buyer, operator, or beneficiary when they differ.  
* Understand the user’s context, tools, skills, preferences, and limitations.  
* Map how the task is performed today, including workarounds.  
* Identify the most consequential friction points in that workflow.  
* Validate important assumptions through available users, credible firsthand accounts, or relevant observations.  
* Avoid treating a few anecdotes as proof of prevalence.

Foundation statement:

“\[Specific user\] struggles to \[specific task\] because \[root cause\]. Existing solutions require \[friction or compromise\]. We enable \[specific improvement\] through \[distinct mechanism\].”

Deliverable: One primary user, one important workflow, and a clearly identified point of failure.

## **4\. Idea Generation and Selection**

* Generate multiple approaches before committing to one implementation.  
* Explore different ways to remove the underlying friction, rather than variations of the same interface.  
* Compare ideas against:  
  * Importance of the problem.  
  * Strength of supporting evidence.  
  * Fit with overall judging criteria.  
  * Fit with the target sponsor challenge.  
  * Meaningful differentiation from existing solutions.  
  * Technical accomplishment achievable during the event.  
  * Clarity and strength of the demonstration.  
  * Availability of necessary data, tools, and hardware.  
  * Dependencies, uncertainty, and likelihood of completion.  
* Identify the assumption most likely to make each promising idea fail.  
* Test major feasibility risks before investing heavily in implementation.  
* Reject ideas whose essential benefit cannot be demonstrated within the event.

Deliverable: A selected concept with a clear reason for choosing it and explicit conditions that would require changing direction.

## **5\. Solution and Feature Definition**

* Define the smallest complete user journey that delivers the promised benefit.  
* Identify the technical centerpiece: the distinctive engineering accomplishment that makes the solution possible.  
* Make the technical centerpiece directly support the user benefit.  
* Define the unique advantage in terms of user outcomes, rather than a list of technologies.  
* Evaluate each feature:  
  * Who needs this?  
  * Does it solve the core problem?  
  * Does it strengthen the demonstration or address a judging criterion?  
  * What does it cost to build, integrate, and explain?  
* Prioritize essential features, supporting features, and optional features.  
* Set explicit cut lines so optional work cannot jeopardize the core experience.  
* Avoid unnecessary infrastructure, integrations, and stack complexity.

Deliverable: A minimum successful build, a technical centerpiece, and a prioritized feature list.

## **6\. Sponsor Challenge Strategy**

* Select a sponsor challenge that naturally fits the project.  
* Identify its exact requirements and any separate judging criteria.  
* Make the sponsor’s technology contribute materially to the solution.  
* Verify access, credentials, quotas, supported capabilities, and integration requirements early.  
* Define what the sponsor integration enables and how we will demonstrate that contribution.  
* Prepare a sponsor-specific explanation that addresses the challenge directly.  
* Maintain a clear overall project narrative even when entering several prize categories.

Deliverable: A sponsor-fit statement, a working integration plan, and evidence that the challenge requirements are met.

## **7\. Technical Architecture and Feasibility**

* Design the simplest architecture capable of delivering the core workflow.  
* Define interfaces, data formats, and responsibilities between components.  
* Identify the critical path and the dependencies that could block it.  
* Test the hardest or least familiar dependency early with a small feasibility prototype.  
* Establish a fallback for essential external services, hardware, or data.  
* Use realistic sample data where appropriate and clearly distinguish sample inputs from live integrations.  
* Address privacy, security, and data handling where they materially affect the project.  
* Make technical choices the team can explain and defend.

Deliverable: A concise architecture, agreed interfaces, and a plan for resolving the largest technical risks.

## **8\. Team Ownership and Time Management**

* Assign clear ownership for:  
  * Core technical system.  
  * Product experience and frontend.  
  * Integrations, data, and testing.  
  * Evidence, pitch, submission, and demo coordination.  
* Treat these as responsibilities rather than rigid job titles; redistribute work when needed.  
* Assign a final decision owner for scope and priority disputes.  
* Establish shared repository conventions, interfaces, and integration procedures.  
* Build an end-to-end version early, even if it is visually rough.  
* Set milestones for feasibility, integration, core functionality, feature freeze, and submission.  
* Reserve explicit time for testing, demo preparation, recording, and rehearsal.  
* When behind schedule, cut optional scope before sacrificing the core workflow.  
* Reassess progress at checkpoints rather than continuously reopening settled decisions.

Deliverable: Named owners, visible milestones, an integration checkpoint, and a feature-freeze deadline.

## **9\. Validation and Evidence of Value**

* Establish what successful performance means for the selected user task.  
* Choose measurements relevant to the actual benefit: time, accuracy, effort, completion rate, reliability, or another appropriate outcome.  
* Compare against a clearly defined existing workflow or baseline.  
* Test the core journey using realistic inputs.  
* Record actual observations and disclose sample size or test limitations.  
* For AI features, test relevant failure cases rather than showing only a favorable example.  
* Separate demonstrated performance from expected future impact.  
* Do not present projected savings, simulated results, or unsupported accuracy claims as measured outcomes.

Deliverable: A small, defensible evidence set showing what works and what improvement was demonstrated.

## **10\. Demo Design and Reliability**

* Design the demo around one clear user story.  
* Show the initial problem, the key interaction, and the resulting improvement.  
* Make the technical centerpiece visible through its effect or a concise supporting explanation.  
* Prepare repeatable inputs, a clean starting state, and a reliable reset.  
* Test the exact device, browser, network, and presentation setup where possible.  
* Prepare responses to likely failures, including unavailable services or unexpected outputs.  
* Have a recorded walkthrough or other permitted fallback.  
* Clearly identify any mocked or simulated components.  
* Practice the demonstration within the allotted time and leave room for judge questions.

Deliverable: A rehearsed live demonstration and a credible fallback.

## **11\. Pitch and Presentation**

Structure the presentation around the strongest evidence:

1. User situation: Introduce a specific person, task, or moment.  
2. Problem: Explain the existing failure and why it matters.  
3. Evidence: Support the problem with relevant facts or observations.  
4. Solution: Explain the core benefit in plain language.  
5. Demo: Show the working user journey.  
6. Technical achievement: Explain the distinctive engineering and important design choices.  
7. Improvement: Show the supported before-and-after comparison.  
8. Differentiation: Explain why this approach improves on the relevant alternatives.  
9. Closing: Reinforce the central benefit in one memorable statement.

Additional principles:

* Use an opening question only when it genuinely strengthens the story.  
* Match technical depth to the judging criteria and audience.  
* Explain what we built, rather than merely listing the tools we used.  
* Include sponsor-specific evidence when presenting for a sponsor award.  
* Use slides only where they improve understanding.  
* Prioritize proof of value and execution over a team slide unless team information is required or directly relevant.

Deliverable: A timed pitch that makes the project’s usefulness, differentiation, and technical achievement easy to assess.

## **12\. Submission and Q\&A Preparation**

* Verify every required submission field, deadline, and prize-category selection.  
* Ensure project descriptions, slides, videos, and repository documentation make consistent claims.  
* Include required source code, setup instructions, architecture information, and demonstration links.  
* Check that links and permissions work for judges.  
* Describe team contributions and external components accurately.  
* Submit early enough to correct preventable issues.

Prepare concise answers about:

* The primary user and the evidence behind the problem.  
* Existing alternatives and our distinct advantage.  
* The hardest engineering problem and how we addressed it.  
* Why the sponsor technology matters.  
* What works today and what remains incomplete.  
* Accuracy, reliability, privacy, cost, and scaling where relevant.  
* The next experiment needed to validate the solution further.

When discussing missing features, explain the scope decision and its implications honestly. Distinguish deliberate exclusions from unfinished work.

Deliverable: A complete submission and a rehearsed set of evidence-backed answers.

**The metrics I would prioritize**

Use these to support the questions above. Avoid producing dozens of scores that look precise but depend on subjective guesses.

| Metric | Definition | Main use |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **First-place rate by attribute** | First-place projects with an attribute ÷ eligible submitted projects with that attribute, within comparable competitions. | Testing whether a characteristic is associated with first place. Requires nonwinner data. |
| **First-place versus finalist difference** | Attribute prevalence among first-place projects minus prevalence among other finalists. | Looking for distinctions among already strong projects. |
| **Cross-event consistency** | Number of independent events where a pattern appears, alongside total events examined. | Separating repeatable patterns from one unusual competition. |
| **General–sponsor overlap** | Projects winning both ÷ general winners eligible for the sponsor award, with entry status recorded where known. | Finding examples of compatible prize strategies. |
| **Time to first demonstrated value** | Seconds until the audience sees a useful working result. | Designing an efficient demo. |
| **Demonstrated completeness** | Critical steps visibly completed ÷ critical steps required for the promised workflow. | Distinguishing a functioning solution from a feature list. |
| **Sponsor integration depth** | Anchored categories: superficial use, useful supporting capability, essential core capability. | Evaluating meaningful sponsor fit. |
| **Evidence coverage** | Projects with adequate source evidence for a finding ÷ projects included in that analysis. | Making missing data visible. |

Always show counts beside percentages: **“4 of 7” is much more informative than “57%.”** Treat associations as associations; better teams may simultaneously produce better demos, deeper engineering, and stronger results.

For subjective attributes, define the scoring criteria before comparing placements. If possible, have two teammates independently assess a subset with award labels hidden. That reduces the tendency to rate everything about a known winner as excellent.

## **3\. What distinguishes sponsor-track first-place winners?**

# **How to turn the analysis into decisions at HackUTD**

Once the actual challenges and rules are available, score each serious idea against the following. I’d use this as a **discussion tool**, not pretend the totals predict victory.

| Criterion | Key question | Weight |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Overall-track strength | Would this be compelling even without sponsor prizes? | 25 |
| Demo and memorability | Can we show a clear, impressive result quickly? | 20 |
| Sponsor fit | Is the sponsor technology essential and visible? | 15 |
| Feasibility | Can our team reliably build the core experience in time? | 15 |
| Problem and user value | Is the problem specific and consequential? | 10 |
| Distinctiveness | Will this stand apart from obvious alternatives? | 10 |
| Additional prize fit | Are other categories a natural fit? | 5 |

Score each criterion from 1–5, multiply by its weight, and divide the sum by 5 for a score out of 100\.

Before scoring, use three gates:

1. **Eligibility:** Does the concept satisfy the event and target-track rules?  
2. **Demo:** Can you describe the exact user action and result you will show?  
3. **Buildability:** Can your four-person team name a plausible path to a working core demo?

If an idea fails any gate, fix it or discard it before letting a high score elsewhere hide the weakness.

The key is to find an idea that is **strong enough to deserve the overall prize on its own**, then identify sponsor and side tracks that naturally reward the same capabilities. Your research should help you recognize that intersection faster—and execute it more convincingly—when the event begins.

## **Clean the sample first**

If you skip this, the rest of the analysis will flatter the wrong projects.

* Tag each row as HackUTD vs other 24-hour university hackathon vs corporate/MLH vs multi-day. Weight HackUTD and similar 24-hour expos highest.  
* Split 1st from 2nd/3rd from honorable mentions. “Winners” as one bucket hides what actually separates first.  
* Record prize type: general, sponsor challenge, best design, hardware, beginner, MLH, people’s choice. A people’s-choice winner is a different product from a sponsor 1st.  
* Drop or down-weight projects that reused a prior hack, were mostly prebuilt, or won on a track you cannot enter.  
* Note team size, hours, and whether a live demo existed. A 48-hour or prebuilt winner is not a build target for Saturday noon to Sunday noon.

## **What actually wins general**

Score every 1st-place general winner, and a sample of 2nds, on the six criteria above, 1–5, from the Devpost writeup and demo video only. Judges will not read your repo.

* **Problem sharpness:** one sentence, a named user, a cost of doing nothing. Vague “AI for X” should score low.  
* **Non-obviousness:** is the twist the data, the workflow, the hardware, or just a ChatGPT wrapper? Count how many 1sts are thin API wrappers versus a real system.  
* **Working core vs feature count:** did 1sts demo one path end-to-end, or a wide unfinished app? Measure “demoable happy path” vs “screenshots of unbuilt features.”  
* **Technical proof in the pitch:** algorithms, integrations, hardware, or a hard constraint they actually solved. Complexity that is not visible in 3 minutes does not count.  
* **Design as a judging lever:** polished UI, one clear flow, accessibility, not a component library dump. Check whether design-track winners also place in general.  
* **Impact beyond the room:** real user, real dataset, or a credible next step. Campus-only jokes win less often than problems a sponsor judge already has.  
* **1st vs 2nd gap:** for paired placings, what did 1st have that 2nd lacked? Usually completeness or story, not a fancier model. That gap is your build priority list.

The finding you want is a minimum bar: “A HackUTD-style general 1st, in this dataset, is a working product that does X, shows Y on screen, and can be explained in Z minutes.” If your shortlist cannot hit that bar by 11 a.m. Sunday, it is not a general-track contender.

## **Sponsor track, then side tracks**

You get two company challenges. A third prize has to be a **HackUTD or MLH side track** that the same build already satisfies. Do not plan on three unrelated products.

For each recurring sponsor-like domain in your data (telecom, banking, energy, insurance, real estate, markets, cloud, maps/spatial, security), answer:

* What did the **challenge statement** actually require, and did the 1st follow it literally or loosely? Literal fits win sponsor tables. Clever adjacent ideas often lose to a boring exact fit.  
* Did the winner **use the sponsor’s API, data, or product**, or only mention the brand? Measure “required tech present in the demo” as yes/no.  
* How crowded was the track relative to prize value? A hard, narrow prompt with few serious submissions beats a glamorous AI track with 40 clones.  
* Could that same project still score on general criteria, or did it become a sponsor checkbox with a weak demo?  
* Which sponsor 1sts also placed in general? That overlap is your target archetype. If the intersection is empty, general 1st and sponsor 1st are in conflict and you must pick a primary.

Side-track questions, same method:

* Which side awards are compatible with a strong general project (design, hardware, data, security) versus mutually exclusive (beginner, meme, pure hardware with no software story)?  
* What was the **minimum extra artifact** that won the side track? Often a tighter UI, a sensor, or a writeup, not a second app.  
* Did MLH or sponsor-booth prizes require presence at a workshop or a specific tool? Those are logistics, not build quality. Put them on a checklist, not in the architecture.

Decision the data should force: rank candidate (problem, sponsor A, sponsor B, side track) tuples by `P(general top) × P(sponsor 1st | we hit the prompt) × buildable in 18 focused hours`. Drop any tuple where one factor is near zero.

## **Judging mechanics, not just ideas**

HackUTD judging is expo-style. Your project is seen by one or more judges, not a long panel. Optimize for a cold 3-minute pass.

From winner videos and writeups, measure:

* Time to first working demo, not time to “motivation” slides.  
* Whether they lead with the problem, show the product, then name the tech, or the reverse. Reverse order loses rooms.  
* Failure rate: did winners have a recorded backup, seeded data, and an offline path? Count how many winning writeups admit the live demo broke.  
* Team role split at the table: one talker, one driver, others silent. Four people talking is a measurable anti-pattern.  
* Devpost quality: screenshot, 60–90 second video, challenge tags, and a description that repeats the sponsor’s words. Judges often open Devpost before they reach your table.

Also pull the negative cases. Projects that looked impressive and still missed 1st usually failed one of: didn’t match the prompt, demo didn’t run, idea was common that weekend, or the story never said who it was for.
