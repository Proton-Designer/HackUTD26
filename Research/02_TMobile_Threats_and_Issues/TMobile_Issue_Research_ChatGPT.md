# T-Mobile Safe Signal Threat and Issue Research

## Research scope

The research follows the uploaded HackUTD 2026 T-Mobile Safe Signal brief, with one deliberate change required by the latest instruction: the original opportunity-mapping section was excluded, so the deliverables identify, verify, compare, and prioritize **issues only**, without proposing solution opportunities. The scope remains limited to telecom carriers, their brands, affiliates, vendors, and attacks involving phone numbers, SIM/eSIM, carrier accounts, telecom infrastructure, carrier employees, or carrier-mediated customer channels. fileciteturn0file0

Primary evidence was prioritized from T-Mobile, the FCC, SEC, FBI/IC3, DOJ, carrier disclosures, and other first-party or regulatory sources. Secondary reporting was used mainly where a primary record did not fully describe attacker behavior or where contemporary reporting preserved claims that were never formally confirmed.

## Most consequential verified findings

T-Mobile's most important recurring security problem is not a single vulnerability class but **multiple paths to customer identity and account control**. The FCC's 2024 investigation documented four major incidents between 2021 and 2023 involving substantially different initial-access mechanisms: compromise of a network environment followed by reconnaissance and credential attacks in 2021; employee-related compromise and SIM-swap/phishing activity in the late-2022 MVNO-platform incident; stolen frontline-sales credentials in 2023; and an API authorization misconfiguration that exposed roughly 37 million accounts. This diversity matters because it shows that the carrier attack surface spans infrastructure, employees, applications, APIs, and telecom identity processes rather than one perimeter. citeturn20search0

The August 2021 breach remains the most consequential T-Mobile data incident in the reviewed period. According to the FCC record, the attacker gained access to a testing environment, conducted reconnaissance, moved laterally, used credential attacks, and ultimately reached customer databases. The exposed population included millions of current customers and tens of millions of former or prospective customers, with sensitive fields including names, addresses, dates of birth, Social Security numbers, and driver's-license information for affected subsets. citeturn20search0 T-Mobile later agreed to a class-action settlement establishing a $350 million settlement fund and committing an additional $150 million toward data-security initiatives. citeturn20search4

The January 2023 incident demonstrated a distinct **API and authorization-control risk**. T-Mobile reported to the SEC that it identified the activity on January 5, 2023, determined that the attacker had begun retrieving data around November 25, 2022, and estimated that approximately 37 million current postpaid and prepaid accounts were affected. The accessible fields included name, billing address, email, phone number, date of birth, T-Mobile account number, number of lines, and service-plan information, although T-Mobile said payment-card data, passwords, PINs, Social Security numbers, driver's-license information, and other government identification were not exposed through that API. citeturn20search3 The FCC later attributed the exposure to a permissions misconfiguration caused by human error. citeturn20search0

Employee identity remains a particularly important control point. The FCC found that T-Mobile's 2023 sales-application incident involved stolen employee credentials and that investigators eventually identified several dozen retail-employee credentials obtained through targeted phishing. T-Mobile initially noticed the issue through an increase in customer port-out complaints, illustrating how compromise of internal employee access can manifest first as customer-facing number takeover rather than as a conventional breach alert. citeturn20search0 Separately, federal prosecutors documented a telecom-store manager who accepted Bitcoin in exchange for performing unauthorized SIM swaps, showing that insider-enabled number takeover is not merely theoretical. citeturn20search12

SIM swaps and unauthorized port-outs deserve high priority because they can convert carrier-account compromise into compromise of unrelated financial, email, social-media, and cryptocurrency accounts that rely on control of the victim's phone number. The FCC's rulemaking record cited documented weaknesses in carrier authentication and customer complaints alleging swaps despite passwords or PINs, including cases involving carrier employees. citeturn20search7turn20search8 The FBI continues to warn that social engineering, phishing, SIM swapping, and call forwarding can be chained to bypass account protections. citeturn20search9

Third-party and cloud compromise is another systemic exposure. AT&T disclosed in 2024 that an attacker unlawfully downloaded customer data from a third-party cloud workspace containing records of calls and texts for nearly all of its wireless customers during specified 2022 and 2023 periods, including interaction metadata and, for a subset, cell-site identifiers. citeturn20search13 Mandiant's investigation of the broader Snowflake-linked campaign attributed many compromises to stolen credentials, often harvested by infostealer malware, and noted that impacted accounts frequently lacked multifactor authentication; Mandiant did not find evidence that the activity resulted from compromise of Snowflake's core platform itself. citeturn20search14 The relevance to T-Mobile is structural: carrier customer data now exists across first-party systems, cloud platforms, SaaS products, contractors, and business partners.

Nation-state intrusion adds a separate class of risk from financially motivated fraud. In November 2024, the FBI and CISA stated that actors affiliated with the People's Republic of China had compromised multiple telecommunications companies, stolen customer call-record data, accessed private communications involving a limited number of individuals primarily associated with government or political activity, and copied information subject to U.S. law-enforcement requests. citeturn20search5 T-Mobile said an intrusion attempt against its environment originated through a connected wireline provider, that its defenses stopped the activity from advancing, and that it found no evidence sensitive customer information had been compromised. citeturn20search6 These two statements are not contradictory: the government described a campaign affecting the telecom sector broadly, while T-Mobile described its own observed exposure.

The evidence also shows that breach announcements can themselves create a secondary fraud surface. Optus explicitly warned customers after its 2022 breach to remain alert for phishing and scams exploiting awareness of the incident. citeturn20search17 In telecom, this post-breach phase is especially important because leaked identity data can make impersonation of a carrier, support representative, bank, or other trusted party more convincing even when the attacker did not obtain a password directly.

A January 2026 T-Mobile breach notification filed with the Maine Attorney General illustrates a narrower but still significant insider/vendor scenario. The filing identifies January 12, 2026 as the incident date and March 5 as the date of discovery, categorizing the event as insider wrongdoing and reporting one affected Maine resident. citeturn20search11 Contemporary reporting citing T-Mobile stated that a vendor employee improperly accessed one customer's information; the limited scale means it should not be conflated with T-Mobile's earlier mass breaches. citeturn20search10

## Risk prioritization

The prioritization report uses four dimensions: **severity, exploitability, prevalence, and customer/downstream impact**. Each is scored on a five-point scale and combined as:

`Risk score = 20 × (0.30 × Severity + 0.25 × Exploitability + 0.25 × Prevalence + 0.20 × Customer/Downstream Impact)`

The highest-ranked issue classes are concentrated around account and number takeover, compromise of privileged employee identities, sensitive bulk-data exposure, third-party/cloud compromise, and nation-state access to telecom infrastructure. The ranking distinguishes between high-frequency fraud affecting individual customers and lower-frequency events whose aggregate or strategic consequences are much larger.

The underlying frequency assessment is supported in part by federal complaint data. The FBI's 2024 Internet Crime Report recorded 982 SIM-swap complaints and 193,407 phishing/spoofing complaints, while total reported cyber-enabled losses reached $16.6 billion across all IC3 categories. citeturn20search10 The 2025 IC3 report recorded more than one million complaints overall and approximately $20.9 billion in reported losses, further indicating that social engineering and account-mediated fraud remain major components of the broader threat environment even though IC3 statistics are not carrier-specific incidence counts. citeturn20search11

## Verification and limitations

Claims were graded by source quality in the verification matrix. FCC orders, SEC filings, DOJ records, FBI/CISA statements, attorney-general notices, and first-party carrier disclosures are treated as the strongest evidence. Secondary reporting is explicitly marked where it contributes information that the primary record does not independently establish.

Several widely circulated claims remain less certain than their repetition online might imply. For example, KrebsOnSecurity reported claims that criminal groups repeatedly accessed T-Mobile employee tools during 2022 and used them in support of SIM-swapping activity; those claims are useful for understanding alleged attacker tradecraft but were not fully confirmed by T-Mobile in the same level of detail, so the catalog marks the unsupported portions accordingly. citeturn20search21 T-Mobile did separately confirm that Lapsus$ obtained stolen credentials and accessed internal operational systems, while saying it found no evidence that customer or government information was stolen in that intrusion. citeturn20search22

The same evidentiary distinction is applied to reports that carrier employees received unsolicited texts offering cash to conduct SIM swaps. Such messages were reported by employees at multiple carriers, but publicly available reporting does not establish the full scale, sender identity, conversion rate, or number of successful compromises; those dimensions therefore remain **UNVERIFIED** rather than being converted into unsupported estimates. citeturn20search24

The resulting research package is therefore intended as an evidence map rather than an exhaustive claim that every telecom incident is publicly visible. Carrier breaches, fraud losses, insider activity, lawful-intercept compromise, signaling abuse, and vendor incidents are often underdisclosed or disclosed only after litigation or regulatory action. The reports distinguish confirmed events, regulator-supported findings, company assertions, credible secondary reporting, and unresolved evidence gaps so that uncertainty is preserved rather than hidden.
